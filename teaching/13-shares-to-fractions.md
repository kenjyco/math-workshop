# From equal shares to points on a line

**What makes a piece one fourth, and where is five fourths?**

## Choose the whole before naming a share

Slide a finger around the edge of one paper disk. Everything inside is the **whole**. In Grade 1, how could you check that a piece is half of it? Turn the shaded piece in the [disk cut through its center](../examples/grade-1/equal-shares.md#two-cuts-through-the-center-of-a-disk) halfway around. It fits the other piece, and together they fill the disk. On the second disk a quarter turn fits the shaded piece into the next space. Four copies fill the disk. A *fourth* and a *quarter* name the same share.

Look for the unequal shares in the [rectangle cuts](../examples/grade-1/equal-shares.md#checking-shares-of-a-rectangle). Both people get one piece, yet one piece covers two little squares and the other six. Equal shares have to cover equal amounts of paper, called **equal areas**. Trace the wholes in the [disk and rectangle drawing](../examples/grade-1/equal-shares.svg). Half of the small four-square sheet covers two squares. A quarter of the big eight-square sheet covers two as well. Saying “half” tells you how its own whole was shared. You need the whole to know how much paper you have.

## Different outlines can share equally

In Grade 2, follow each [closed outline and the unfolded cube](../examples/grade-2/shape-outlines.svg). To [draw from a description](../examples/grade-2/shapes-and-shares.md#drawing-from-the-attributes), trace one straight side per turn until you're back at the start. Three, four, five and six sides give a triangle, quadrilateral, pentagon and hexagon. A cube needs six square **faces**, flat surfaces. Fold the four-square belt and close its two ends to account for faces hidden in the sketch.

Lay two identical rectangles together before cutting. Now inspect the [middle cut and diagonal cut](../examples/grade-2/shapes-and-shares.md#halves-that-take-different-shapes). Each sheet gives two pieces that fit each other after a slide or turn. Each pair rebuilds an entire sheet. A rectangular half and a triangular half therefore cover the same amount of paper even though their outlines differ. In the [circle and strip partitions](../examples/grade-2/equal-partitions.svg), check that all shares fit without gaps or overlaps. A *third* is one of three equal shares. Recover a whole by [putting together two halves, three thirds or four fourths](../examples/grade-2/shapes-and-shares.md#naming-the-shares-and-the-whole).

## Count spaces from zero

Grade 3 gives these shares number names. The [6-by-2 sheet](../examples/grade-3/shares-and-jumps.md#one-whole-sheet) has three matching squares. One occupies four of the twelve square centimeters. Its share is 1/3. The denominator, 3, says how many equal shares fill one whole. A single such share is a *unit fraction*. The numerator, 2 in 2/3, counts the shares taken.

On the [two number lines](../examples/grade-3/shares-and-jumps.svg), the new whole is a **length**, the unit interval from 0 to 1. Put a fingertip on 0. You haven't traveled any distance yet. Move to the next mark. Now you've traveled one sixth on the [line with seven marks](../examples/grade-3/shares-and-jumps.md#six-spaces-between-seven-marks). Six spaces need a start plus six landings. Three jumps take you halfway across. On the [line in quarters](../examples/grade-3/shares-and-jumps.md#a-fifth-quarter-jump), keep the jump size fixed even after you reach 1. Four jumps use the whole unit. The fifth goes one quarter farther, so 5/4 is beyond 1. If a label goes missing, start at zero and count the spaces you cross.

## Hold the endpoint still

Ask someone to hold a finger at the end of the shading in the [aligned fraction strips](../examples/grade-3/fraction-strips.svg). [Cut every third in two](../examples/grade-3/names-for-one-point.md#split-each-third-in-two), including the unshaded third. The whole now has six equal pieces and your finger is four pieces from zero. The same distance has two *equivalent* names, 2/3 and 4/6. The first third alone has become two sixths. Remove the halfway marks in reverse to check. On the [whole-number landings](../examples/grade-3/names-for-one-point.md#when-a-fraction-lands-on-a-whole-number), eight quarter jumps fill two whole units. A denominator of one makes each jump a whole unit, so 3/1 lands at 3.

For the [comparisons on one scale](../examples/grade-3/names-for-one-point.md#which-way-does-the-sign-point), ask what stays fixed. Five eighths goes farther than three eighths because the steps match. Two thirds goes farther than two fourths because the same two steps are longer in thirds. Record 3/8 < 5/8, 2/3 > 2/4 and 2/3 = 4/6. The half-ribbons measure 3 cm and 2 cm because their wholes differ. Grade 3 uses halves, thirds, fourths, sixths and eighths here, plus whole-unit jumps.

Build on [parts that reassemble a whole](03-parts-and-equality.md) and [measuring spaces between marks](05-measuring-length.md). [Joining shapes](02-what-makes-a-shape.md) can help you picture the sheet. Carry the fixed endpoint back to [renaming fractions for arithmetic](14-fraction-arithmetic.md#keep-the-endpoint), where you'll also use multiplication, to [tenths and hundredths](15-cents-and-hundredths.md), which uses base-ten units, or to [fractional measurements on a plot](17-fractional-line-plots.md), which also uses frequency counts.
