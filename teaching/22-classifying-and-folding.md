# Which names and folds fit a figure?

**What must be true of the whole figure for a name or a fold to fit?**

## Let a figure keep every name it earns

In Grade 3 trace each of the [four outlines](../examples/grade-3/four-outlines.svg). Four straight sides close up in each, so all are *quadrilaterals*. In the [grid tests](../examples/grade-3/four-sided-figures.md#four-closed-outlines), A has four right angles, making it a rectangle. B has four equal sides, the test for a rhombus, **and** four right angles. That makes it a square and gives it both names. Turn B until one edge is level and repeat both tests. C's sides match, but the narrow tip fits inside a square corner with room left. D's top and bottom lengths differ and its right side slants. To draw a quadrilateral outside those special groups, copy D's three-step top and four-step bottom, with their left ends aligned. Join the ends and test its sloping side. Neither C nor D earns the square's full set of names. Use [defining features of shapes](02-what-makes-a-shape.md) if their orientation is distracting.

## Rotate the corner test with the triangle

In Grade 4 a *right triangle* needs one right angle. At the bottom of A on the [two triangle grids](../examples/grade-4/triangle-tests.svg), walk four steps right and three up along one side's direction. A left quarter-turn sends that direction three left and four up, along the other side. At B's bottom vertex the other side reaches three left and **five** up. Its endpoint falls inside the quarter turn. Use a paper corner at all three vertices when [sorting the tilted figures](../examples/grade-4/sorting-and-folding.md#g60--sort-by-the-actual-corners). B has three acute angles. A and the rectangle qualify for “has perpendicular sides.” Only the rectangle qualifies for “has parallel sides.” Turn the page and the corner together to check. This step uses [measuring a turn](21-measuring-turns.md).

## Follow one corner and its two edges

In a *reflection*, points on the fold line stay fixed. Every other point travels straight across at a right angle, the same distance beyond the line as it started from it. An *axis of symmetry* is a fold line that matches the entire figure. Ask a parent to name one corner and follow both adjoining edges as you [test the folds](../examples/rectangle-reflection.md#test-the-folds). On the [four-by-two rectangle](../examples/rectangle-reflection.svg), the horizontal midline swaps top and bottom edges, each one unit from the crease. The vertical midline swaps left and right edges, each two units from that crease. In both cases the other sides fold onto themselves and the interiors match.

Across diagonal AC, corner A stays fixed. B is four units from A. The opposite corner D is only two units from A. A fold keeps distances, so B must miss D. Follow the perpendicular through B to see it land beyond the bottom edge. The halves have equal area, four square units each, but the edges miss. To [check for any other crease](../examples/rectangle-reflection.md#check-for-other-folds), follow the long top edge. If its ends swap on that edge, the crease must be the vertical midline. Fixing both ends would crease the top edge and send the figure outside. If the edge swaps with the bottom, their midpoints must swap, forcing the horizontal midline. That's exactly two axes. Compare the [half-turn that swaps the diagonal pieces](../examples/rectangle-reflection.md#parent-bridge) with a fold that keeps A and C still.

## How can you finish the search?

Look at the [square and scalene triangle](../examples/grade-4/square-and-scalene.svg) while [testing the whole boundary](../examples/grade-4/sorting-and-folding.md#g61--test-an-entire-boundary). The square's two midlines swap opposite edges. Along either diagonal the other two corners swap while the ends of the crease stay fixed. For any further crease, the center must stay put and A must land on a corner. Keeping A fixed gives its diagonal through the center. Sending it to any of the other three corners forces the crease halfway across that trip, perpendicular to it. Those three creases are already counted. Draw each surviving crease through the center and test the endpoints of one edge. Four axes exhaust the possibilities.

The triangle's sides measure 3, 4 and 5 units. Such a triangle is *scalene*, with all side lengths different. A reflection would have to send each side onto itself because no other side has its length. Then each meeting point of two sides would stay fixed. No straight crease passes through all three corners, so it has zero axes. For optional help with equal area, [rebuild two half-sheets](13-shares-to-fractions.md#different-outlines-can-share-equally) or [trace the boundary of a covering](12-area-and-perimeter.md). Return to the corner test to decide whether a fold works.
