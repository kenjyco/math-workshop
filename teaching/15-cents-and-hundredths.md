# Cents, tenths and hundredths

**How can two different counts name the same amount?**

## What can you buy back with the change?

In Grade 2, start with [adding and removing parts](03-parts-and-equality.md). A coin's *denomination* tells its value. In the [two payments for a stamp](../examples/grade-2/money-exchanges.md#two-ways-to-pay-for-a-stamp), set one dime aside in each payment. Three dimes on one side can buy exactly what the quarter and nickel on the other can buy: thirty cents. Restore the dime on each side. Both payments now buy the 40¢ stamp. Counting coins or comparing their sizes would miss that equality. A dime is smaller than a nickel yet worth ten cents to its five.

In the [purchase with a dollar](../examples/grade-2/money-exchanges.md#a-purchase-with-a-dollar-bill), keep the dollar as the money whole. One dollar can be exchanged for 100 cents. The stamp and 42¢ postcard cost 82¢ together. *Change* is the money returned when the payment exceeds the price. From 82¢, three pennies take you to 85¢, a nickel to 90¢ and a dime to 100¢. Those returned coins total 18¢. A $1 bill covers the whole purchase. Putting price and change together checks that the entire bill is accounted for. You can do the whole story in cents.

## Give the grid its own whole

Grade 4 now chooses **one complete grid** as one unit. The reasoning uses [places](06-bundles-and-trades.md) and [renaming equal fractions](14-fraction-arithmetic.md#keep-the-endpoint). Find three full rows in the [hundred-cell grid and line](../examples/grade-4/hundred-grid.svg). A *tenth* is one of ten equal rows. A *hundredth* is one of the hundred equal cells. In the [grid calculation](../examples/grade-4/decimal-places.md#g53--a-grid-and-a-unit-interval), rename the three rows as thirty cells before adding seven more individual cells. Thirty-seven cells give 37/100. Count the other way. Three spaces at the end of the fourth row and sixty below it leave 63 empty. That leaves exactly 37 filled.

The *decimal point* separates the ones place from the fractional places. In 0.37 the 3 counts tenths and the 7 counts additional hundredths. To [recover the point on a line](../examples/grade-4/decimal-places.md#read-the-point), keep the 0-to-1 length as one whole and walk thirty-seven hundredth-steps. You pass 0.3 and stop short of 0.4.

## Does the seven count rows or cells?

Ask a parent to cover the labels and pose that question for the [three decimal readings](../examples/grade-4/decimal-places.md#compare-the-places). Seven rows cover seventy cells, so **0.7 = 0.70**. The *trailing zero* records zero additional hundredths. The 7 stays in tenths. In 0.07 the empty tenths place leaves just seven cells. Compare 0.7 with 0.68 by reading both in hundredths. From 68, two more steps reach 70. Write 0.07 < 0.7 and 0.70 > 0.68. More written digits do not tell you which point is farther from zero. Keep the whole the same throughout. To [combine money amounts in a measurement story](16-measurement-units.md), also identify the unit the question requests.
