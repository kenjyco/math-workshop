# Copies, factor pairs and growing patterns

**How can a grouping explain a comparison or predict something we have not yet counted?**

## What changed when your finger moved one cell?

In the [Grade 3 addition and multiplication tables](../examples/grade-3/tables-and-tens.md#two-tables-with-different-steps), follow the row for four. Moving right one column adds one to an **addend** in the addition table. In the product table it adds a **whole group of four**. Predict the entry after sixteen without looking: one more four makes twenty. Split each four into two pairs. Any whole number of groups of four can be paired completely, which explains why the entries are even no matter how far you extend the row. For help, [turn and split an array](09-equal-groups.md) or [match partners](01-counting-a-collection.md).

In the [packets of forty](../examples/grade-3/tables-and-tens.md#six-bundles-of-forty), treat one rod as a ten. Six packets of four rods give 24 rods. That's 24 **tens**, or 240 ones. Untie the rods to check the unit. This Grade 3 strategy multiplies a one-digit number by a multiple of ten from 10 through 90. [Bundling](06-bundles-and-trades.md) is a useful model if the zero is puzzling.

## Choose the reference amount

Grade 4 asks “times as many as **what**?” In the [bars starting at the same point](../examples/grade-4/bead-bars.svg), the reference amount is Inez's four beads. The [two comparisons](../examples/grade-4/bars-factors-and-patterns.md#g43--which-amount-is-the-reference) both use “three.” Three **copies** of four give twelve beads. Three **extra beads** beyond four give seven. Ask someone to cover the original four on each longer bar. Two whole copies remain on the first, three individual beads on the other.

Hide Inez's count. Three equal parts of twelve give four beads in the reference bar. Hide the multiplier instead. Count how many four-bead lengths fit into twelve. The answer “three times” counts copies. Substitute it into the original comparison to check: 12 = 3 × 4 says that twelve beads are three times as many as four.

## Know when the rectangles run out

A *factor pair* gives two whole-number side counts whose product fills a rectangle. For [targets from 1 through 100](../examples/grade-4/bars-factors-and-patterns.md#g44--when-is-the-pair-list-finished), try the shorter side first. For 36, lengths 1, 2, 3 and 4 fit with partners 36, 18, 12 and 9. Five leaves one object after seven full rows. Six fits with six. A new rectangle with **both** sides at least seven would need at least 49. A rectangle with a shorter side already tested just turns a known pair. That proves the list of five pairs is finished.

A *multiple* of nine is a whole number of nines, so 36 qualifies. No number of fives makes 36. A *prime* has exactly two distinct positive factors, as 17 does. A *composite* has more than two, as 36 does. One has only the factor one, so neither name fits.

## Predict the next leftover

A *rule* tells how to build the next *term*, a member of a pattern. [Start at one and add three](../examples/grade-4/bars-factors-and-patterns.md#g45--what-keeps-alternating). The *parity* of a number tells whether it's odd or even. Three new counters bring one ready-made pair and a single. That single either finds the old leftover or becomes the new leftover. So 16 leads to odd 19, then even 22. Append three squares to the growing strip and test the same prediction at its end. To [choose between comparison and other operations in a story](20-multistep-models.md), you'll also need to follow the question through several steps.
