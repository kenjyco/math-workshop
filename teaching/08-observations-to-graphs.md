# From observations to graphs

**How do the marks let us recover the counts that made them?**

## Give one visit one mark

In Grade 1, watch for **visits**. The same bird could visit twice. Each visit is an *observation*, one thing recorded. In the [feeder list](../examples/grade-1/bird-counts.md#from-feeder-visits-to-three-columns), slide a finger from visit 6 to the robin column. The visit number goes with it, so you can put it back in order. Each kind is a *category*. Its *frequency* is its count. The four robins, two wrens and four finches account for ten visits. [Pair the columns](../examples/grade-1/bird-counts.md#how-many-more-or-fewer) across. Two robin marks have no wren partners, while every robin mark has a finch partner. This uses [matching and sorting](01-counting-a-collection.md).

## Ask two questions of the same strips

In Grade 2, [measure each of seven strips](../examples/grade-2/strip-graphs.md#measuring-the-sample-strips) to the nearest whole centimeter. See how B ends just before 6 and C just after in the [common-start drawing](../examples/grade-2/sample-strips.svg). Both round to 6 cm. On the [line plot and material graphs](../examples/grade-2/lengths-and-materials.svg), a *line plot* places one mark for each measurement over its value. [Read the stacks](../examples/grade-2/strip-graphs.md#one-cross-per-measured-strip) by naming a length once per cross. One each at 3 and 4, two each at 6 and 7, and one at 8 gives seven lengths. Leave 5 on the scale. Its empty stack says zero strips of that measured length.

Now cover the length column and [sort by material](../examples/grade-2/strip-graphs.md#two-graphs-of-the-materials). Three paper, two felt, one foil and one cork give seven strips again. A picture stands for one strip here. A *bar* shows the count by the distance its end reaches from the starting line or *baseline*. Combining paper and felt gives five. Matching the three paper with the two felt leaves one extra paper. The graphs keep the counts. Without the labeled source rows, they cannot tell you which length belonged to a particular material.

## Check the key before counting

Grade 3 changes what a mark is worth. Make a *tally*, one count for each letter in the [sightings record](../examples/grade-3/birds-in-scaled-graphs.md#the-observation-record). Ask a parent to touch a single dot in the [scaled picture and bar graphs](../examples/grade-3/bird-graphs.svg) and find the letters it stands for. The *key* says two sightings per dot. [Read from zero](../examples/grade-3/birds-in-scaled-graphs.md#reading-the-two-scales): four robin dots represent eight sightings, then three, two and one dots give six finches, four wrens and two jays. Reporting four robins would leave four robin letters unaccounted for.

To build the graphs yourself, draw one dot per pair of sightings or extend a bar one two-sighting interval per pair. The finch and jay bars differ by two intervals, four sightings. For the [combined comparison](../examples/grade-3/birds-in-scaled-graphs.md#comparing-bar-heights), eight robins plus four wrens make twelve. Match six finches against that combined count. The excess of six spans **three** two-sighting bar intervals. A graph without visit numbers cannot recover the original sighting order. [Equal spaces on a ruler](05-measuring-length.md) help with the scale. To [explain the key with equal groups](09-equal-groups.md), you'll use repeated addition. To recover fractional lengths from stacks, you'll also need fractional ruler intervals.
