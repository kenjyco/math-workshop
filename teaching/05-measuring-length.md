# Measuring the space between two ends

**Why can two ruler readings tell us one length?**

## Line up the starting ends

Which book is bigger? Choose the *attribute*, the feature you want to compare. In the [Kindergarten book comparison](../examples/kindergarten/length-and-weight.md#two-comparisons-with-the-same-book), set the bottom ends of the spines against a ledge. The far end of the picture book sticks out, so its spine is longer. On a balance that starts level, the dictionary's pan sinks. Swap pans to check. The longer book is lighter. On the [felt and card strips](../examples/kindergarten/length-and-weight.md#when-the-ends-meet), line up the near ends first. Matching far ends show equal lengths even if the balance tips.

In Grade 1, what if you can't move the shelf to the ribbon? [Carry the three lengths](../examples/grade-1/length-strips.md#carrying-lengths-to-one-strip) to one strip by marking each endpoint from the same start. The cord reaches past the ribbon and falls short of the shelf. That puts the ribbon first and the shelf last. [Compare the tile runs](../examples/grade-1/length-strips.svg) below those marks. A *unit* here is the width of one tile. Only five touching, nonoverlapping tiles cover the strip once from end to end. The gap includes space no tile covers. The overlap counts the same space twice. Try tracing the [five widths](../examples/grade-1/length-strips.md#when-five-tiles-measure-the-strip) with a finger.

## A ruler remembers all the tile ends

In Grade 2, an *estimate* uses a familiar length to predict a measurement. Compare each [estimate with its tool reading](../examples/grade-2/measuring-length.md#choosing-a-tool-and-testing-an-estimate). A ruler fits the page, a yardstick or meter stick can be moved end to end along a long straight span, and a tape follows the curved trim. Think in inches for the page, feet for the shelf, meters for the room. Compare the estimated 8 inches, 5 feet and 6 meters with the measured 9 inches, 4 feet and 5 meters in the record. The estimate of three ten-centimeter strips gives 30 cm for the trim. Following the curve gives 28 cm, two centimeters less. Say the unit along with the number.

Look at the [two scales over one strip](../examples/grade-2/ruler-spans.svg). Each centimeter contains ten millimeter *intervals*, spaces between marks. [Six centimeters and sixty millimeters](../examples/grade-2/measuring-length.md#two-scales-on-the-same-strip) therefore reach the same endpoint. Ask a parent to touch both ends of the [lower strips](../examples/grade-2/measuring-length.md#starting-at-the-two-mark). From 2 cm to 8 cm the first centimeter is already finished when your finger reaches 3. Subtract the start to count all six spaces. The other strip spans 2 to 6, four centimeters. Their far tips are two centimeters apart. Adding those two to four checks the longer length.

## The seam is a place to start again

Follow the [two lengths of binding](../examples/grade-2/length-jumps.md#two-pieces-of-binding) and the [arrows at the seam](../examples/grade-2/binding-line.svg). The first eight centimeters reach the seam. Five more take you to the 13 cm mark. On the [return arrow](../examples/grade-2/length-jumps.md#from-the-seam-to-the-end-and-back), begin at 13 and move five one-centimeter spaces back. You land at 8. The mark 13 names a position measured from zero. The movement from the seam measures only five centimeters.

Start here with direct comparison. [Ordering collections](01-counting-a-collection.md) is optional help for “more” and “less.” The same idea of intervals helps with [clock readings](07-clock-readings.md), which use two moving hands, fractions on a line, which use equal shares, and fractional plots, which also count repeated measurements. To choose units for other attributes, bring multiplication and division for the later conversions.
