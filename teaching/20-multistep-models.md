# Which quantity answers the question?

**When is a correct calculation only a step toward the answer?**

## What does the letter count?

A mathematical *model* keeps the relationships that matter to the question. A *variable* is a letter standing for a quantity. Before writing an equation, say what you want the letter to count. In Grade 3 the [stamp sheet](../examples/grade-3/two-step-stories.md#stamps-left-on-a-sheet) starts with thirty stamps. If R counts the stamps **remaining**, R = 30 − 3 × 4. Multiply first because each envelope takes four. Twelve stamps went onto envelopes. That leaves eighteen on the sheet. Putting those twelve back recovers thirty.

In the [four garden beds](../examples/grade-3/two-step-stories.md#seeds-for-four-beds), each bed gets *both* kinds of seeds. The parentheses in 4 × (8 + 3) gather one bed's eleven before counting its four copies. The estimate of forty, based on ten per bed, misses just one seed per bed, giving 44 altogether. For the [cards on one table](../examples/grade-3/two-step-stories.md#cards-for-a-table), first share 36 among six tables. Then give the two spares to the specified table. Its eight cards can be checked by setting two aside and repeating the original six-card share six times. These two-step problems use whole-number answers. Use [the role of an unknown](04-additive-stories.md), [equal groups](09-equal-groups.md) and [rounding for estimates](11-rounding-and-columns.md) to choose and check the operations.

## Read the unit after the subtotal

For the Grade 4 [shipment, prices and rental](../examples/grade-4/shipment-and-capacity.md#g49--follow-the-units-to-the-question), ask a parent to read the unit after each intermediate quantity. Then reread the last sentence. “24 clips **per carton**” is a complete packing instruction. Call the number of shipped clips C. One pack has six clips, four packs make a carton, and five cartons arrive. C = 5 × (4 × 6) = 120 clips. Reverse it: 120 divided among five cartons gives 24 per carton. Dividing 24 among four packs gives six per pack. The 30 cm carton length supplies no clip count.

Which price question are you answering? At equal counts, eight pads save eight copies of the $3 price difference. That's $24, checked by subtracting the $48 bill from the $72 bill. At equal budgets of $36, compare **counts** instead. Six lower-priced pads and four higher-priced pads give a difference of two pads. In the rental, 32 guests need eight tables at four seats each. At $7 per table, the requested **cost** is $56. Eight correct tables on a paused page tell you where to resume. Multiply by the rent, then divide the bill by eight to recover the quoted rate.

## Test the last seat

*Capacity* is how many can fit. Bring the result of [1,205 divided by four](19-quotient-places.md#read-each-place-as-an-amount-per-share) to the [vehicle question](../examples/quotient-zero-and-capacity.md#find-the-vehicles-needed). The remainder of one counts a rider. For the **fewest** vehicles that seat everyone, test neighboring capacities. With 301 vehicles, 301 × 4 = 1,204 < 1,205. With 302, 302 × 4 = 1,208 ≥ 1,205. One vehicle fewer fails, so 302 is enough and is the least. At the *threshold*, the rider count that exactly fills 301 vehicles, all 1,204 riders fit. Three hundred vehicles would seat only 1,200.

For 247 jars in cases of six, 240 and 300 are both easy to divide by six. The estimate using 240 misses by seven jars, compared with 53 for the other estimate. Even the closer estimate cannot tell you the least sufficient order. Forty-one cases hold 246 jars. One more case gives 252 spaces. Compare the two capacities again if you're unsure what to do with the leftover jar.
