# Parts, tens and two sides of an equation

**How can moving part of a group make a sum easier and keep it true?**

## Put the buttons back

Start with [one touch per button](01-counting-a-collection.md). In Kindergarten, to *join* two groups, bring them together. To *separate* a group, move some away. In the [dish story](../examples/kindergarten/joining-and-splitting.md#buttons-into-and-out-of-a-dish), four buttons join six. Slide three of the ten onto a cloth. The seven in the dish and the three on the cloth still account for ten. Tip out the last seven. How many are in the dish now?

In the [splits of seven](../examples/kindergarten/joining-and-splitting.md#one-whole-and-two-parts), point to the whole group as you say “seven” and to its parts as you say “two and five.” Read 7 = 2 + 5 in either direction. Move one button from the five to the two. Now write 7 = 3 + 4. Each part changed, and the whole stayed put. In the [facts within five](../examples/kindergarten/joining-and-splitting.md#related-facts-within-five), use the very same split to undo a sum. If one and four make five, taking four back leaves one. Taking all five leaves zero.

## Listen for what ten means

Seven counters in a frame leave [three empty spaces](../examples/kindergarten/tens-and-teens.md#how-many-more-will-fill-the-frame). Three is the *complement*, the part needed to make ten. Start with any of the frame counts from one to nine. Write the occupied count plus the empty count and check that their sum is ten. On the [teen frames](../examples/kindergarten/tens-and-teens.md#how-many-beyond-a-full-frame), the frame is already full. For sixteen, touch the six **extra counters** outside it. Ten individual ones and six more ones give sixteen. Move those six to the other side. You still have sixteen. This works from eleven to nineteen.

In Grade 1 the amounts being joined are called *addends*. Find the eight counters in the [frame with six beside it](../examples/grade-1/making-ten-and-equality.md#from-eight-and-six-to-ten-and-four). Before filling a space, ask someone to point to the group that will supply the two counters. Trace those two from the six into the frame. Eight gets two, six loses two, and the mat holds ten and four. Reading the mat in the opposite order changes which addend you say first. Grouping the same counters differently leaves the sum fourteen. Try reversing a removal instead: you need enough counters in the starting group to remove the requested amount.

## Read both sides

An *expression* such as 8 + 6 names an amount. “Equals” says two amounts are the same. Test the [two proposed equalities](../examples/grade-1/making-ten-and-equality.md#what-the-equals-sign-says) by covering one side at a time. **8 + 6 = 16 − 2 is true** because the frame gives fourteen and taking two from sixteen also gives fourteen. **13 − 8 = 2 + 4 is false** because the left side gives five and the right side gives six. You can check the five by [counting on from eight](../examples/grade-1/making-ten-and-equality.md#counting-moves-and-finding-a-missing-part). Touch one finger for each move to thirteen. Eight is the starting point, so the first move lands on nine.

In the [facts within ten](../examples/grade-1/making-ten-and-equality.md#facts-within-ten), four plus five is one more than double four. Nine minus five checks the result. Grade 1 uses these facts in work within twenty. When a box stands for an *unknown*, ask what part is hidden. In the [missing-number records](../examples/grade-1/making-ten-and-equality.md#which-number-belongs-in-the-box), put four back with seven to recover the start in □ − 4 = 7. Substitute eleven for the box and take four away to test it.

To [find an unknown in a story](04-additive-stories.md), also identify who has each group. To [make a bundle of ten](06-bundles-and-trades.md), keep track of which sticks the bundle contains.
