# Renaming, joining and repeating fractions

**What can we change about a fraction while preserving its length?**

## Keep the endpoint

Cover the numbers beside the [strips that each measure one meter](../examples/grade-4/same-meter.svg). Where does the shading stop? Use the [earlier equal-share explanation](13-shares-to-fractions.md#hold-the-endpoint-still) to check why adding marks can leave this point fixed. Back at the [meter divided into thirds](../examples/grade-4/fraction-strips.md#g50--subdivide-and-compare), cut **every** third into two, including the unshaded third. Four smaller pieces reach as far as two old ones and six fill the whole. Split again to get eight twelfths. Removing the new cuts recovers two thirds. Multiplying the numerator and denominator by the same positive whole number records that action.

A *benchmark* is a known amount you can compare with. In the [two comparisons](../examples/grade-4/fraction-strips.md#two-ways-to-compare), half a meter separates 3/8 from 2/3. Four eighths would reach halfway, while four sixths pass three sixths. A *common denominator* gives both lengths the same counting unit. Each quarter holds three twelfths and each third holds four. Thus 3/4 covers nine twelfths and 2/3 covers eight. Read the endpoints as 3/8 < 2/3 and 3/4 > 2/3. A shared endpoint takes the equals sign. Grade 4 uses denominators 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 100 in this work.

## Cut a length and put it back

In the [joined quarter-meter pieces](../examples/grade-4/fraction-strips.md#g51--join-separate-and-exchange), the fifth piece has the same length as the first even though it lies beyond one meter. In the [two cuts of five eighths](../examples/grade-4/fraction-strips.md#two-cuts-in-five-eighths), slide the cut from just after the first piece to just after the second. Record 5/8 = 1/8 + 4/8 and 5/8 = 2/8 + 3/8. Moving the cut changes the two counts but keeps the full five pieces. Each side of each cut is still measured in eighths. Adding like units counts the pieces, so the denominator stays eight.

Lay a three-eighth-meter strip against the end of a two-eighth-meter strip. Check that they touch without overlapping. Five equal eighths span the joined length. Removing the last two recovers the first three. A *mixed number* counts wholes and a fraction of another whole. In the [lengths joined and cut](../examples/grade-4/fraction-strips.md#lengths-joined-and-cut), 1 3/4 m and 1 2/4 m give two wholes and five fourths, or three wholes and a fourth. Now start afresh with **2 1/4 m** and cut off **1 3/4 m**. Before cutting, can you show three loose fourths? Ask someone to open one whole meter into four fourths. With the fourth already there you have one whole and five fourths. Take away one whole and three fourths. Two fourths remain. Lay the cut length back: seven fourths and two fourths restore nine fourths, the original length. The [bundle trade](06-bundles-and-trades.md#trade-and-return) is an optional model of the same exchange idea.

## Count pieces inside each person's length

In the [marked lengths](../examples/grade-4/copies-of-a-length.svg), five copies of a quarter-meter piece give five fourths. A *whole-number multiple* counts how many copies of a length you take. [Bracket the copies](../examples/grade-4/fraction-strips.md#g52--count-copies-of-a-measured-length) and then look at the [cord for three people](../examples/grade-4/fraction-strips.md#cord-for-three-people). Two fifth-meter pieces belong to **each** person. Three people need three pairs, six fifths of a meter. Five fifths fill the first meter and the last fifth begins the next. The 3 counts people, 2/5 m measures each person's cord, and 1/5 m is the length of one piece.

This uses [joining parts](03-parts-and-equality.md) and [equal groups](09-equal-groups.md). To [add tenths and hundredths](15-cents-and-hundredths.md), use the ten-by-ten grid as the whole. For [ribbon sums on a plot](17-fractional-line-plots.md), also read how many observations occupy each tick.
