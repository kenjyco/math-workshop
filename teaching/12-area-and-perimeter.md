# Covering a floor and walking its boundary

**Why do two measurements of the same garden have different units?**

## What does one tile measure?

Put a fingertip inside a tile on the [tiled paper and stepped floor](../examples/grade-3/tiles-and-floor.svg). Now walk it along one edge. The first touch picks out surface. The second traces length. In Grade 3, *area* measures the interior by how many copies of a *unit square* cover it. Each tile on the [paper rectangle](../examples/grade-3/tiles-and-floor.md#a-square-to-measure-a-surface) is one centimeter on a side and covers one **square centimeter**. Keep neighbors touching edge to edge. For the hearth use square feet. For the tray use identical square paper stamps. Each covers one unit of the chosen kind. A gap leaves paper unmeasured, and an overlap counts some paper twice. Look at the [different tile choices](../examples/grade-3/tiles-and-floor.md#what-changes-with-the-tile): six square meters of patio and six square inches of card share a count but use very different squares.

Use [equal rows](09-equal-groups.md) and [side lengths](05-measuring-length.md) to read the [product on the paper](../examples/grade-3/tiles-and-floor.md#a-product-you-can-cover). Four one-centimeter widths fit across, and three one-centimeter heights fit down. Every row can hold four squares, so three rows give twelve square centimeters. Split off one column. Each of the three rows still contributes one plus three squares. That explains why the two smaller products add to the original area.

## Change the cut or walk the fence

A *rectilinear* floor has straight sides meeting at right angles here. In the [two cuts of the stepped floor](../examples/grade-3/tiles-and-floor.md#two-cuts-of-a-stepped-floor), hide the alcove. Twelve square feet remain visible, and the alcove supplies four more. Start over and hide the top row. The two full rows below have six squares each, and the top row has four. Both cuts give **16 square feet**. Every square has a home in exactly one part. The seam is just a line.

Ask a parent to trace the [outside of bed A](../examples/grade-3/garden-beds.svg) while you count its four sides. The *perimeter*, the distance once around, is fourteen meters. In the [missing-side walk](../examples/grade-3/fences-and-coverings.md#one-trip-around-the-garden), eleven meters are already accounted for, so the last three meters close the walk. A line between neighboring tiles has garden on both sides and needs no fence. Test the [two pairs of beds](../examples/grade-3/fences-and-coverings.md#equal-fences-and-equal-coverings). A and B each use 14 m of fence but enclose 12 and 10 m². A and C each enclose 12 m² but use 14 and 16 m of fence. Either measurement alone leaves room for different rectangles.

## Work backward from the measure you have

Grade 4 writes the row argument as area = length × width. Opposite sides match, so perimeter = 2 × (length + width). On the [garden grid](../examples/grade-4/garden-grid.svg), one row along the six-meter length covers six square meters. In the [garden with known area](../examples/grade-4/rectangle-measures.md#g57--recover-a-side-from-a-measure), 24 m² needs four such rows. Each adds one meter to the width. Walk the four outside edges: 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 gives **20 meters of edging**. For the other garden, remove the two 7 m lengths from 22 m of perimeter. The remaining 8 m must pay for **two** equal widths, so each is 4 m.

To [split a larger multiplication rectangle](18-partial-products.md), also bring place value. To [test a fold](22-classifying-and-folding.md), use corresponding edges and corners as well as area.
