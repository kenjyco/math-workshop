# Counting a collection

**How can we tell how many there are when the objects move?**

## Give each button one word

In Kindergarten you can start with number words. Say “thirty-seven.” Can you keep going when the row runs out? At forty, your finger goes to the next row and your voice says forty-one. You can say the words even with your eyes shut. On the [number path to 100](../examples/kindergarten/counting.md#two-ways-to-reach-100), the last word in each row tells you how far ten more steps have taken you. Start at one and read every cell to count by ones to 100. Then read just the row ends to count by tens to the same hundred. Try the [short stretch starting at 37](../examples/kindergarten/counting.md#start-at-37) by ones.

Now open your hand and count some buttons. Each touch gets one word. Once every button has had a turn, the last word tells **how many altogether**. That's the total, or *cardinality*. Look at the [seven buttons with letter names](../examples/kindergarten/counting.md#seven-buttons-then-one-more). Ask someone to spread them out. Find button A again. It has a new neighbor, but it's still the same button. Each of the seven can go back to its old spot. Predict what you'll say on the last touch. Now give a **new** button a turn. All seven are still there, so the next word, eight, tells the new total.

## Where should my finger stop?

Open the [four layouts in the counting record](../examples/kindergarten/counting.md#count-a-layout-or-make-a-collection) and [follow the marked starting counter](../examples/kindergarten/counting-layouts.svg). The ring around the top counter is a reminder. Say “one” there. Walk your finger around the circle one counter at a time. When the next touch would be the marked one, lift your finger. That counter already had its word! Go the other way from the mark. The first and last neighbors trade jobs and you still get twenty touches. In a line the end tells you to stop. In four rows of five, finish each row before starting another. For the ten scattered counters, move each one to a dish as you say its word. An empty mat tells you you're done.

What if the mat was empty to begin with? There is nothing to touch. Write **0**. A *numeral* is a written name for a number. Cover a label on the [mats from zero to twenty](../examples/kindergarten/counting.md#write-how-many) and write the total yourself. Making a collection has a different stopping signal. If someone wants twelve buttons, stop moving them on “twelve,” even with more in the dish. Check the twelve in two rows of six.

## Give each spoon a partner

Which row has more? Put one spoon in each of the [four widely spaced cups](../examples/kindergarten/comparing-and-sorting.md#match-across-two-groups). A *pair* here has one of each. Two of the six spoons still need partners. That tells us there are more spoons and fewer cups. Add two cups, match again, and every object has a partner. The counts are equal. Try matching groups with up to ten objects on each side. A parent can squeeze the cups closer together and ask, “Which spoon lost its partner?” Check each pair together.

The [number cards](../examples/kindergarten/comparing-and-sorting.md#read-the-numeral-cards) can stand for counts even when the cups are put away. Eight comes before ten, so eight is fewer. Turn the cards around and read the comparison from the other side. Two eights name equal counts. Try this with cards from 1 to 10. To [sort the stamped tiles](../examples/kindergarten/comparing-and-sorting.md#sort-a-handful-by-its-markings), give each tile one home according to its mark. Each kind is a *category*. Pair the three moons with the three suns. All match. The four stars would need one more partner. We use at most ten tiles of any one kind here. Three is less than four. That puts the two tied categories first when we order by count.

You can start here with objects and number words. For a later use of pairing, [find the extra objects in a story](04-additive-stories.md) uses addition and subtraction. To turn one observation into one mark, you'll also need to keep each kind in its own category.
