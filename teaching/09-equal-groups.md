# Equal groups, arrays and missing factors

**What changes when we know a total but a grouping quantity is missing?**

## Touch a dot, then touch a space

In Grade 2, [make partners for nineteen counters](../examples/grade-2/pairs-and-arrays.md#a-partner-for-each-counter). Nine pairs leave one counter reaching for a partner. We call nineteen *odd*. Add one and all ten pairs match. Twenty is *even*: each of its two rows has ten, so 20 = 10 + 10. This test works through twenty, including zero, where nothing is left without a partner. [Counting and matching](01-counting-a-collection.md) can help.

An *array* arranges objects in equal rows and columns. [Turn three rows of four](../examples/grade-2/pairs-and-arrays.md#rows-that-turn-into-columns). Each old column becomes a row, so you can count the same twelve by fours or threes. Grade 2 uses arrays up to five by five and writes the counts as equal addends, for instance 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. Find three different things in the [counter and tile drawing](../examples/grade-2/arrays-and-cells.svg): a dot, a tile interior and a crossing of lines. In the [tiled rectangle](../examples/grade-2/pairs-and-arrays.md#squares-inside-the-rectangle), draw three rows and four columns of equal squares so that the rows fill the rectangle. Three rows of four interiors give twelve squares. Five vertical lines each meet four horizontal lines, giving twenty crossings. Where you put your fingertip decides what you've counted.

## What does the missing number count?

Grade 3 calls the numbers multiplied *factors* and their result the *product*. In the [four bags](../examples/grade-3/bags-rows-and-lengths.md#four-bags-of-buttons), say both units: four bags, six buttons per bag. Their product counts all 24 buttons. Now [deal or pack the same 24](../examples/grade-3/bags-rows-and-lengths.md#sharing-or-packing-the-same-buttons). When six people are waiting, one round uses six buttons. Four rounds give **four buttons per person**. When each bag needs six buttons, each finished bag uses six, so you can fill **four bags**. The arithmetic looks identical on paper. The question tells you what four means.

Try the [pegboard and ribbon](../examples/grade-3/bags-rows-and-lengths.md#rows-on-a-pegboard-and-pieces-of-ribbon). On the ribbon, 30 cm divided into five equal pieces gives 6 cm per piece. Packing 30 cm into pieces 6 cm long gives five pieces. Multiply back with the units to find the original length. In the [six equal trays](../examples/grade-3/bags-rows-and-lengths.md#six-equal-trays-holding-forty-two), 42 is the *dividend*, the total, and six the *divisor*, here the number of trays. Write 6 × □ = 42. Five shells on each tray use thirty, and two more on each use the last twelve. Substitute seven in the box. The *quotient* is seven shells per tray because six sevens use 42 shells. Exchanging 42 and 6 would start with only six shells to share among 42 trays. Switching the operands changes the question.

## Keep track of every row

In the [turned, bundled and split groups](../examples/grade-3/turning-and-splitting-arrays.md#turn-bundle-and-split), turning the array exchanges factors. Counting two boxes of three sleeves before counting four counters per sleeve changes the grouping. Both counts still reach 24. Ask someone to cover the three right columns of the seven-by-eight array. The part under the hand still has **all seven rows**, so it hides seven threes. Combine that with seven fives to recover 56. Splitting one factor and multiplying both parts is the *distributive property*.

Use the [inverse readings](../examples/grade-3/turning-and-splitting-arrays.md#reading-back-from-a-product) to recover either positive side of an array from the total and the other side. In the [product table](../examples/grade-3/turning-and-splitting-arrays.md#the-one-digit-product-table), a zero row gives zero objects, and one row gives exactly its row size. Dividing by a positive heading undoes multiplying by it. Grade 3 works with products or dividends within 100 and the recall set of one-digit products. For help with the row sums, revisit [joining addends](03-parts-and-equality.md). To [list factor pairs](10-factors-and-patterns.md), also use exact division. To [measure a covering](12-area-and-perimeter.md), give each cell a square unit. To rebuild written product rows, you'll also need tens and ones.
