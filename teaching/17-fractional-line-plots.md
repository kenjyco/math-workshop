# Reading lengths back from a line plot

**What can a stack of marks tell us about both length and frequency?**

## Look sideways for the length

In Grade 3 put a finger at zero on the [strip rulers and plot](../examples/grade-3/strips-and-plot.svg). The *fractional scale* divides an inch into equal parts. On the [two rulers](../examples/grade-3/strip-lengths.md#read-from-the-origin), three half-inch intervals and six quarter-inch intervals end at the same length, 1 1/2 inches. Strip B and strip E both need a mark there. Giving them one shared mark would lose a strip. The letters name different objects, even when the readings match.

Cover the measurement table and [recover the observations](../examples/grade-3/strip-lengths.md#rebuild-the-list-from-the-plot). Read left to right, saying the length once for every cross. You get one strip at 1 inch, two at 1 1/4, two at 1 1/2 and two at 2 inches. The seven observations account for the seven strips. Where is 1 3/4? Its tick is still one quarter inch beyond 1 1/2 and has no cross. Removing that space would change the scale. A parent can move a finger sideways one tick, then up one mark in a stack. Which movement changed the measurement? Going up changes only the *frequency*, how many times that length occurred.

## Two questions at the same plot

Grade 4 measures in halves, fourths and eighths. Read the [ribbon stacks](../examples/grade-4/ribbon-plot.svg) using one eighth of a yard per interval. Make the plot by marking eighths from zero and placing one cross for each ribbon, stacking whenever a tick is already occupied. On the [four ribbon measurements](../examples/grade-4/measuring-and-plotting.md#g56--which-marks-belong-to-which-ribbons), assign a length to each cross. One is at three eighths, two at four eighths and one at six eighths. Four eighths is half a yard. Six eighths is three quarters. Reading the horizontal position this way prevents the taller stack from looking like a longer ribbon.

For the **combined length** of the two half-yard ribbons, imagine laying the two ribbons end to end. Four eighths plus four eighths fills one yard. For the **difference** between extreme lengths, line up the starts instead. From the end of the shortest to the end of the longest takes three one-eighth intervals, giving 3/8 yard. Adding that difference to 3/8 reaches 6/8 again. If you lose the question, return to the four assigned lengths and choose which ribbons the words describe.

Use [spaces between marks](05-measuring-length.md), [unit fractions](13-shares-to-fractions.md#count-spaces-from-zero) and [like-unit sums](14-fraction-arithmetic.md) for the measurements. For a sideways check, [compare the two strip displays](08-observations-to-graphs.md#ask-two-questions-of-the-same-strips) and explain what each mark counts before returning to the ribbons.
