# Birds at the feeder

Grade 1 · Organizing and comparing category counts

Each observation belongs to one of three categories.

## From feeder visits to three columns

**[1.MD.C.4](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/1/MD/C/4/).** A watcher writes down one bird name for each visit. Which birds visited most often? Here is the original list in order. The numbers identify visits. A bird that comes back makes another visit.

```text
 1 robin       2 wren       3 finch       4 robin
 5 finch       6 robin      7 wren        8 finch
 9 robin      10 finch
```

Move each visit number into the column for its bird. Each numbered mark below represents one observation. The **frequency** of a category is the number of marks in its column.

| Robin | Wren | Finch |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| ● 1 | ● 2 | ● 3 |
| ● 4 | ● 7 | ● 5 |
| ● 6 |   | ● 8 |
| ● 9 |   | ● 10 |

How many visits of each kind? Reading down gives **4 robin, 2 wren and 4 finch visits**. Robins and finches tie for the most. How many visits altogether? 4 + 2 + 4 = 6 + 4 = **10 visits**.

Check the display in reverse. Its columns give four “robin” labels, two “wren” labels and four “finch” labels. Return each label to its numbered place. Reading positions 1 through 10 recovers the original list, with each visit used once.

## How many more or fewer?

Pair the first two robin marks with the two wren marks across their rows. The robin marks for visits 6 and 9 have no partners. That gives **two more robin visits than wren visits**, or **two fewer wren visits than robin visits**. Each finch mark has a robin partner, so the difference between those columns is zero.

**Parent bridge.** The unmatched marks explain 4 − 2 = 2. Starting with four robin visits, pairing off one for each wren visit leaves exactly the two visits counted by “how many more?”
