# Which quantity does the box stand for?

Grade 2 · G17 · Concepts `model`, `add`, `equal` · MP1. [Goal and standards, p. 19](../../goals/grade-2.md#g17-mental-facts-and-one--and-two-step-additive-stories)

## Facts within twenty

**2.OA.B.2.** Eight counters are on the mat and seven are beside it. How many altogether? Move two of the seven to the eight to **make ten**. Now 8 + 7 = (8 + 2) + 5 = 10 + 5 = 15. Taking the seven back leaves eight. A **known sum** gives another route. Double seven is fourteen, so one more counter makes fifteen. In reverse, 15 − 8 asks for the part that joins eight to make fifteen. That part is seven.

Mental fluency means finding sums and differences within 20 accurately and readily. For example, 13 + 7 = (10 + 3) + 7 = 10 + 10 = 20, so 20 − 7 = 13. These two problems are within the mental range. The narrower year-end **recall** target is to know every sum of two one-digit numbers from memory. The complete set is below. A child's own responses over varied cases provide evidence of fluency.

Choose the first addend at the left, the second at the top. Their cell gives the sum. Reading back from a cell after taking away its row or column heading gives **both inverse differences**. For the cell at row 0, column 9, the two readings are 9 − 0 = 9 and 9 − 9 = 0. At row 9, column 9, both readings give 18 − 9 = 9.

## A tray of tickets

**2.OA.A.1.** Each question below starts a fresh story. The sketch shows how the quantities fit together once the box is filled. A bundle holds ten tickets, a dot is one loose ticket. A box in an equation stands for the count the question asks for. [Additive situations, Table 1, p. 88](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=88)

```text
One part                 Other part         Both parts together
[bundle] [bundle] ••••   •••••• •••         three bundles and three loose
       24                     9                         33
```

Six loose tickets complete a third bundle with the four already on the tray, leaving three loose. In reverse, taking three loose tickets and six from a bundle removes nine.

| Story and question | Equation with the unknown | Answer in the story |
|---|---|---|
| Add to 24 tickets by dropping in 9. How many now? | 24 + 9 = □ | 24 + 6 + 3 = 33 tickets now. |
| Start with 24. Some arrive, bringing the count to 33. How many arrived? | 24 + □ = 33 | Six to reach 30 and three more give 9 arrivals. |
| Add 9 to a tray and count 33. How many were there at first? | □ + 9 = 33 | 33 − 3 − 6 = 24 tickets at first. |
| Take 9 from 33. How many stay? | 33 − 9 = □ | 33 − 3 − 6 = 24 tickets stay. |
| Take some from 33 and leave 24. How many went out? | 33 − □ = 24 | The gap from 24 to 33 is 6 + 3 = 9 tickets taken. |
| Take 9 tickets and leave 24. How many were there at first? | □ − 9 = 24 | Put the nine back. The start was 33 tickets. |
| A tray holds 24 adult and 9 child tickets. How many tickets? | 24 + 9 = □ | Join the parts in the sketch to get 33 tickets. |
| Of 33 tickets, 9 are child tickets. How many are adult tickets? | □ + 9 = 33 | Set the nine aside. The other 24 are adult tickets. |
| Of 33 tickets, 24 are adult tickets. How many are child tickets? | 24 + □ = 33 | The other 9 are child tickets. |

**Both parts unknown.** Four tickets go into a left envelope and a right envelope. How many could go in each? Write 4 = □ + △. Move one from right to left each time. In left/right order the possible counts are (0, 4), (1, 3), (2, 2), (3, 1), (4, 0). Each rebuilds four and the left count takes every possible value.

**Comparing two trays.** The upper tray has a matched part and an extra part. Lay out the lower tray directly beneath the matched part.

```text
Upper    [24 matched tickets] [9 extra]
Lower    [24 matched tickets]
```

| Given and asked | Solve for the box |
|---|---|
| Upper 33, lower 24. How many more on the upper tray, or how many fewer on the lower? | 24 + □ = 33. The unmatched 9 give both differences. |
| Lower 24. Upper has 9 more than lower, or equivalently lower has 9 fewer than upper. How many on the upper tray? | □ − 9 = 24. Restore the extra part. 24 + 9 = 33 upper tickets. |
| Upper 33. Lower has 9 fewer than upper, or equivalently upper has 9 more than lower. How many on the lower tray? | □ + 9 = 33. Remove the extra part. 33 − 9 = 24 lower tickets. |

## Two steps at the ticket window

**2.OA.A.1.** A window sells 24 tickets before lunch and 9 afterward. Another window sells 40. **How many more tickets did the other window sell?**

```text
First window     [24 before lunch][9 after]
Other window     [same subtotal           ][extra]
```

1. **How many did the first window sell?** Its subtotal is 24 + 9 = 33 tickets.
2. **How many more did the other window sell?** Write 33 + □ = 40. The known sum 3 + 7 = 10 takes 33 up to 40. The other window sold **7 more tickets**.

Work backward. Remove seven from forty to recover thirty-three. Remove the afternoon nine from that subtotal to recover the morning twenty-four.

**Parent bridge.** Write “first window, 33 tickets” beside the first calculation. The original question asks for the extra tickets at the other window. The second label, “7 more tickets,” answers it.

A missing part can also feed the next step. Of 15 advance tickets, 8 are adult tickets and the rest are child tickets. Another 12 child tickets sell at the door. How many child tickets sell altogether? First, 8 + □ = 15 gives **7 advance child tickets** from the known sum. Then 7 + 12 = 7 + 10 + 2 = **19 child tickets altogether**. To check, 19 − 12 = 7 and 7 + 8 = 15.
