# What does four count?

Grade 3 · G27 · `groups`, `product`, `divide`, `model`, `equal` · MP2 · [Goal and source, p. 23](../../goals/grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g27-products-both-meanings-of-division-and-missing-factors)

## Four bags of buttons

Each pair of brackets holds one bag. Each dot is one button.

```text
[● ● ● ● ● ●]   [● ● ● ● ● ●]   [● ● ● ● ● ●]   [● ● ● ● ● ●]
```

The **factors** tell us there are four bags and six buttons in each. The **product** answers “How many buttons altogether?” Counting the bags in turn reaches 6, 12, 18, 24. Thus 4 × 6 = □ gives **24 buttons**.

## Sharing or packing the same buttons

Share all 24 buttons equally among six people. Deal one button to each person per round. Four rounds use 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24. Each person gets **4 buttons**. Here 24 ÷ 6 = 4 gives the **size of one share**. Six shares of four reconstruct the 24 buttons.

Now pack those same buttons six to a bag. Close a bag at 6, 12, 18 and 24 buttons, as in the drawing. Here 24 ÷ 6 = 4 gives the **number of bags**. Four full bags of six reconstruct the 24 buttons. If the packed tray has four bags and we have forgotten how many went into each, 24 ÷ □ = 4. Substituting 6 buttons per bag gives 24 ÷ 6 = 4 bags.

**Parent bridge.** Ask “Four of what?” In 6 × □ = 24 the missing four counts buttons for each person in the first story. In the second story it counts bags, each contributing six buttons.

## Rows on a pegboard and pieces of ribbon

```text
Pegboard             Ribbon laid along a centimeter scale
● ● ● ● ●            0     6     12    18    24    30
● ● ● ● ●            |-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
● ● ● ● ●
```

Each peg occupies one position. Three rows of five give 3 × 5 = **15 pegs**. If only the total and the three rows are known, put one peg in each row five times. 3 × □ = 15 gives **5 pegs per row**. If each row holds five, □ × 5 = 15 gives **3 rows**, checked by 5 + 5 + 5 = 15.

On the ribbon, each space between consecutive labels is one 6 cm piece. Five pieces laid end to end give 5 × 6 = **30 cm**. Cut that length into five equal pieces and each measures 30 ÷ 5 = **6 cm**. Cut it into 6 cm pieces instead and the five intervals give 30 ÷ 6 = **5 pieces**. Both cuts use 5 × 6 cm = 30 cm.

## Six equal trays holding forty-two

There are 42 shells on six equal trays. In 42 ÷ 6, **42 is the dividend**, the total being shared. The **divisor** is the six trays. Find the missing factor in 6 × □ = 42. Five shells on each tray use 30. Two more on each use 12. All 42 are placed, so 42 ÷ 6 = 7. The **quotient** counts **7 shells per tray**. Multiply back by counting seven lots of six to reach 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42.
