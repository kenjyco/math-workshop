# Why is every fourth count even?

Grade 3 · G29 · `pattern`, `laws`, `place`, `product` · MP8 · [Goal and source, pp. 23, 24](../../goals/grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g29-explaining-patterns-in-tables-and-products-of-tens)

## Two tables with different steps

Each entry combines its row heading with its column heading.

| + | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| 3 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| 4 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |

Moving right adds one to the second addend, so the sum increases by one. Going down one row while moving left one column keeps the sum fixed, as in 2 + 4 = 3 + 3 = 4 + 2 = 6. One addend gives the other one unit. Extend the row for 4 to column 5 and the next sum is 9.

| × | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
| 3 | 0 | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 |
| 4 | 0 | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 |

In the row for 4, moving one column right adds a new group of four. The next entry is 16 + 4 = 20. Why must any whole number of fours be even? Split each four into two pairs. Put one pair from every group on the left and one on the right. The two piles match, so each counter on the left has a partner on the right. Even zero groups leave no counter unpaired. Beyond these columns, eleven fours split into 22 + 22 = 44. The same split works for any number of groups.

## Six bundles of forty

One packet holds four rods, each worth ten ones. Six packets give 6 × 4 = **24 rods**, each still worth one ten. Thus 6 × 40 ones = 24 tens = **240 ones**. Counting in ones gives the successive totals 40, 80, 120, 160, 200, 240.

**Parent bridge.** Bundle twenty of the tens into two hundreds. Four tens and zero loose ones remain. That is what each digit in 240 records.
