# Grade 4 · Whole numbers and their applications

Throughout the base-ten domain, whole numbers are at most 1,000,000. Addition and subtraction require the standard algorithm. Multiplication and division require a strategy grounded in place value, properties or the relationships specified below. For these two operations, equations, rectangular arrays and/or area models illustrate and explain the calculation. [CCSS Mathematics, grade section](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29)

## G41. Reading, comparing and rounding through one million

Concepts `place`, `product`, `order`, `round`. [G18](grade-2.md#g18-making-hundreds-counting-in-increments-and-comparing) supplies place units, [G29](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g29-explaining-patterns-in-tables-and-products-of-tens) the tenfold relationship and [G30](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g30-computing-and-estimating-with-whole-number-place-value) rounding. A digit's value is its count times its place unit. Moving a digit one place left multiplies its value by ten.

| Standard | Grade expectation | Selected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [4.NBT.A.1](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | A digit has ten times the value it has one position to the right. | Compare the values of the 7s in 770. Verify 700=10 × 70. |
| [4.NBT.A.2](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | Read/write base-ten numerals, words, and expanded forms and compare using >, =, <. | Use internal zeros and include 1,000,000. Reconstruct the numeral and compare the highest differing place. |
| [4.NBT.A.3](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | Use place value to round whole numbers to any place within scope. | Include a midpoint, unchanged exact multiple, and a carry to a new place. Verify neighboring multiples. |

**Examples.** Use the two 7s in 770, multi-digit numbers with internal zeros, expanded form, words, and the endpoint 1,000,000. Compare by the highest place of difference and round whole numbers to any specified place within scope.

**Independent check.** MP7. Independently reconstruct each numeral from its expanded form. For rounding, locate adjacent multiples, test both sides of a midpoint, an exact multiple and a carry into a new place. Keep operands and results within 1,000,000.

**Parent bridge.** After the adjacent 7s, connect ten units in the right-hand position to one unit in the position to its left.

## G42. Recording exchanges in standard addition and subtraction

Concepts `exchange`, `add`. [G19](grade-2.md#g19-adding-partial-sums-and-exchanging-across-a-zero) supplies regrouping and [G41](grade-4-whole-numbers.md#g41-reading-comparing-and-rounding-through-one-million) the place columns. One unit carried to the left has the value of ten units in the column where it arose. Unbundling a unit adds ten in the place to its right.

| Standard | Grade expectation | Selected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [4.NBT.B.4](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | Fluently add and subtract multidigit whole numbers using the standard algorithm. | Show aligned columns, including regrouping across zeros. Verify every trade and the inverse calculation. |

**Examples.** Specify fluent standard-algorithm examples for both multidigit operations through 1,000,000. Include a cascading carry and subtraction across several zeros. Place-value diagrams can explain selected rows.

**Independent check.** MP6. Independently evaluate the value before and after each trade. Verify a difference by adding back and recompute a sum with regrouped partial sums. Check alignment of unequal-length operands.

**Parent bridge.** After the zero-crossing example, explain the changes in each written column through equal-value exchanges.

## G43. Comparing by a factor and comparing by a difference

Concepts `product`, `model`, `divide`. Use [G09](grade-1.md#g09-finding-the-unknown-in-an-additive-story) for additive comparison and [G27](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g27-products-both-meanings-of-division-and-missing-factors) for factor and quotient roles. The reference amount is the amount multiplied. The multiplier describes how many times as large the compared amount is.

| Standard | Grade expectation | Selected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [4.OA.A.1](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | Read a product equation as a multiplicative comparison and translate a spoken comparison into an equation. | Identify the reference quantity, multiplier, and compared quantity. Check the equation in both directions. |
| [4.OA.A.2](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | Solve multiplication/division comparison stories and distinguish multiplicative from additive comparison. | Contrast “three times as many” with “three more.” Vary which quantity is unknown and check against the wording. |

**Examples.** Translate a multiplication equation into a verbal comparison and reverse the translation. Pair “three times as many” with “three more.” Use a labeled bar drawing and an equation with an unknown for each multiplicative role, including an unknown reference and an unknown multiplier.

**Independent check.** MP4. Rebuild bar lengths independently of the equation. Check the number of equal reference lengths and substitute each answer back into the comparison sentence. A multiplier can count copies while the two compared quantities carry the same unit.

**Parent bridge.** After the paired bars, show where the difference appears and where one repeated reference amount appears.

## G44. Finding every factor pair and recognizing multiples

Concepts `factor`, `product`, `divide`. [G27](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g27-products-both-meanings-of-division-and-missing-factors) supplies factor, product and exact division. A positive factor divides a number exactly. A multiple of a number is the product of that number and a whole-number multiplier. Prime and composite describe numbers with exactly two or more than two positive factors respectively.

| Standard | Grade expectation | Selected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [4.OA.B.4](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | For 1–100, find every positive factor pair, relate a number to its factors as a multiple, test whether the number is a multiple of a given one-digit number, and classify prime/composite where applicable. | Include 1, a prime, a square, and 100. Verify exhaustive pairs and explain why 1 has neither classification. |

**Examples.** Within 1–100 find every positive factor pair for chosen numbers including 1, a prime, the square 36 and 100. Decide if a number is a multiple of a given one-digit number and explain how every factor makes the original number its multiple.

**Independent check.** MP3. Independently test each positive candidate d while d × d is at most the target. Once d × d exceeds the target, two partners both at least d would already give too large a product. Check each product, count a square pair once, and account for rejected candidates. For 1 verify the single distinct factor and its classification as neither prime nor composite. If the proposed one-digit factor is 0, check whether any whole number times 0 gives the positive target.

**Parent bridge.** After the pairs for 36, rotate a rectangle to show why reversing the dimensions does not give a new unordered pair.

## G45. Generating a pattern and explaining an unstated feature

Concepts `pattern`. Begin with a rule and an initial term. [G20](grade-2.md#g20-pairs-equal-addends-and-square-arrays) supplies parity and [G29](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g29-explaining-patterns-in-tables-and-products-of-tens) illustrates property-based explanations. A term is one member of the pattern. A rule determines how to produce terms.

| Standard | Grade expectation | Selected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [4.OA.C.5](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | Generate a number or shape pattern from a rule, identify features the rule does not state, and explain their persistence informally. | Start at 1 and add 3, or repeat a shape construction. Separate the generating instruction from the observed feature. |

**Examples.** Generate a number or shape pattern from the given rule. Use the number pattern starting at 1 and adding 3. Record both the instruction and an observable feature that the instruction does not explicitly name.

**Independent check.** MP8. Independently apply the rule for a fresh term. Explain why an odd increment reverses parity at every step, so the pattern continues past the displayed terms.

**Parent bridge.** After the terms, connect the alternating feature to paired counters and the one unpaired counter in an odd increment.

## G46. Accounting for every partial product

Concepts `multi-product`, `laws`, `place`. [G41](grade-4-whole-numbers.md#g41-reading-comparing-and-rounding-through-one-million) supplies place values, [G27](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g27-products-both-meanings-of-division-and-missing-factors) whole-number products, [G28](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g28-deriving-facts-from-multiplication-properties) distribution, and [G19](grade-2.md#g19-adding-partial-sums-and-exchanging-across-a-zero) addition of place-value parts. [G32](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g32-measuring-with-square-units-and-adding-rectangle-areas) supports the area representation. A partial product multiplies one part of one factor by one part of the other.

| Standard | Grade expectation | Selected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [4.NBT.B.5](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | Multiply up to four digits by one digit, and two digits by two digits. Use place value and operation properties. Illustrate and explain using equations, rectangular arrays, and/or area models. | Include both operand shapes, internal zero, and the four-region rectangle. Reconcile every partial product. |

**Examples.** Include both required operand shapes. For 23 × 14 use a labeled area rectangle, an expanded equation and two vertical rows. For 2,036 × 4 retain the internal zero place. Include equivalent partitions and a nearby expression with one region omitted or counted twice. The choice of four regions and of a stacked bridge is editorial.

**Independent check.** MP7 and MP1. Independently check factor sums, one occurrence of every pairing, the value of each region, then the sum of all region values. Require paired records isolating a correct split with a wrong region value even when the final answer agrees, all correct products with a wrong sum, and correct rows left unsummed. Contrast a corrected calculation with an incomplete one. If the products are absent the first faulty arithmetic step is unknown. Compare source factors and the independently recomputed product with the final answer field.

**Parent bridge.** After the area model, group the four regions into 23 × 4 and 23 × 10 and align their stacked rows by value. Check the total independently using 23 × (15 − 1). Standard multiplication algorithm fluency belongs to grade 5.

## G47. Counting groups removed in partial quotients

Concepts `multi-div`, `divide`, `place`. [G41](grade-4-whole-numbers.md#g41-reading-comparing-and-rounding-through-one-million) supplies place units, [G27](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g27-products-both-meanings-of-division-and-missing-factors) supplies both division meanings and the inverse product, and [G19](grade-2.md#g19-adding-partial-sums-and-exchanging-across-a-zero) supports subtraction. A partial quotient counts groups. The amount removed is the divisor times that count.

| Standard | Grade expectation | Selected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [4.NBT.B.6](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=30) | Find whole-number quotients and remainders for up to four-digit dividends and one-digit nonzero divisors. Explain using place value, properties and/or the inverse relationship, with equations, arrays, and/or area models. | Use two valid chunk sequences and verify the running group count and amount remaining at each removal. |

**Examples.** For whole dividends of up to four digits and positive one-digit divisors, specify exact and nonexact divisions with equations and a group ledger. For 84 ÷ 4 accept repeated friendly groups (10, 10, 1) and a larger first chunk (20, 1). For 2,487 ÷ 6 include a line removing 600 with 100 groups and a contrasting line crediting only 10 groups for that same removal. Also include correct individual group counts with an incorrect final sum. Give amount removed, groups represented, amount remaining and running total groups on each row. A correct shorter or longer sequence is equally admissible.

**Independent check.** MP1. Check each removal against its group count and each subtraction against the previous remaining amount. At every row require original dividend = divisor × accumulated groups + amount remaining. Only the final remainder must be smaller than the divisor. Recompute the final quotient by a different grouping. Compare the source operands, scratch result and transcribed answer separately. A cramped, unfinished record should identify the last valid row and next possible move. Group counts can be built from known facts, with no extended fact table required.

**Parent bridge.** After two valid paths, relate their summed group counts to the hundreds, tens and ones counted in conventional long division. Distinguish subtracting a multiple of the divisor from recording the number of groups it represents.

## G48. Writing quotient digits in their places

Concepts `multi-div`, `divide`, `place`. Use [G41](grade-4-whole-numbers.md#g41-reading-comparing-and-rounding-through-one-million) for place, [G27](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g27-products-both-meanings-of-division-and-missing-factors) for equal sharing and inverse multiplication, and [G19](grade-2.md#g19-adding-partial-sums-and-exchanging-across-a-zero) for exchanges. [G47](grade-4-whole-numbers.md#g47-counting-groups-removed-in-partial-quotients) offers another representation of division. The current amount after an exchange and the digit in the quotient have different meanings.

| Standard | Grade expectation | Selected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [4.NBT.B.6](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=30) | Find whole-number quotients and remainders for up to four-digit dividends and one-digit nonzero divisors. Explain using place value, properties and/or the inverse relationship, with equations, arrays, and/or area models. | Use place-unit labels and the conventional layout to verify quotient digits, including a zero, against the original dividend. |

**Examples.** For dividends of up to four digits and positive one-digit divisors, include 1,205 ÷ 4 and a case such as 2,436 ÷ 6 in which 3 tens remain to be shared among 6 groups at the tens step. Contrast a current entry written “03” with 30, and check the units before bringing down the ones. Label the place of each current amount. Distinguish a zero left by subtraction, the next input digit and a zero required in the quotient. Include exact hundreds divided into equal groups and name the quotient unit before converting to ones.

**Independent check.** MP6. Independently reconstruct both dividends by divisor × quotient + remainder with 0 ≤ remainder < divisor. Check an omitted zero and a shifted digit against both a magnitude bound and reconstruction. Include a correct scratch quotient copied incorrectly and the converse, a correct selection with inconsistent scratch work. State what each record establishes. In a stopped example identify the unprocessed digit and allow continuation on a new line.

**Parent bridge.** After the place-value model, map each exchange to the next digit brought down in a conventional layout and each allocated place to its quotient column. Show why the place unit changes while the remaining quantity is conserved. Flexible group removal is another valid route.

## G49. Choosing operations and interpreting what remains

Concepts `model`, `multi-div`, `round`. [G31](grade-3-operations-and-area.md#g31-representing-two-step-whole-number-problems) supplies two-step modeling and estimates, [G43](grade-4-whole-numbers.md#g43-comparing-by-a-factor-and-comparing-by-a-difference) comparison, [G46](grade-4-whole-numbers.md#g46-accounting-for-every-partial-product) products and [G47](grade-4-whole-numbers.md#g47-counting-groups-removed-in-partial-quotients)–[G48](grade-4-whole-numbers.md#g48-writing-quotient-digits-in-their-places) quotients and remainders. An intermediate answer has a unit and may answer a different question from the final unknown. A compatible number is a nearby input chosen to make the estimate easy to calculate.

| Standard | Grade expectation | Selected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [4.OA.A.3](https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf#page=29) | Solve multistep whole-number stories with whole-number answers across the four operations. Interpret remainders, represent the unknown by a letter, and assess reasonableness mentally or through estimates including rounding. | Record intermediate quantities with units, an equation, and a context-specific final answer. Check the estimate and the remainder decision. |

**Examples.** Across multistep whole-number stories use all four operations, a letter for the unknown and whole-number answers. In one shipment story, give packs per carton, pieces per pack, number of cartons and an irrelevant length. In two further stories, find the per-item difference between prices before using the item count, and the number of tables before using the cost per table. Pair a correct per-carton subtotal with an incorrect claim that it is the shipment total. Contrast equal spending at different unit prices with equal counts.

**Independent check.** MP2 and MP4. Independently derive the unit chain and operation for every step from the requested quantity, substitute into the equation, and check mental estimates including rounding. For 1,205 riders with four available passenger seats per vehicle, distinguish full vehicles, leftover riders and vehicles needed. Verify capacity for the proposed count and one fewer. For 247 ÷ 6 compare two easy dividends, 240 and 300. Judge closeness by distances from 247, label the estimates “about,” and test an exact capacity threshold separately. Include an exact-division case where no extra container is needed.

**Parent bridge.** After each paired story, label the intermediate and final quantities in words. After the capacity case, connect the numerical remainder to the decision about one more vehicle.
